Through a Sister's Eyes

A Testimony of Faith, Family, Love, and Never Giving Up Hope

Sometimes the hardest battles we face in life aren't our own. They're the battles we watch someone we love fight, knowing that no matter how desperately we want to help, we cannot fight those battles for them. For me, one of those battles has been watching my brother struggle with addiction. Our family has always been incredibly close. We were raised with love, faith, and the understanding that family stands together through life's storms. We weren't a perfect family, but we loved one another deeply. That love was something my mom and dad instilled in us, and it's something I've carried with me throughout my life. Over the years, I've experienced more loss than I ever imagined I would have to endure. I've said goodbye to my mom, my dad, and two of my sisters. Each loss left an emptiness that can never truly be filled. So when addiction began threatening my brother's life, my fear was about so much more than addiction. I was terrified of losing another piece of my family. My brother. And twice, I came frighteningly close to losing him. There is a kind of fear that comes with loving someone who struggles with addiction that is difficult to explain unless you've lived it. The sleepless nights, the unanswered questions, the constant worry, and the dread that the next phone call might bring news you never wanted to hear. I have prayed prayers through tears. I've experienced anger, disappointment, exhaustion, and moments when I simply didn't know what else to do. I've tried to help him out of difficult situations. I've worried about whether I was making the right decisions. I've questioned whether I was helping him or unintentionally making things easier for the addiction to continue. And I've felt the helplessness of realizing that no matter how much I loved him, I could not make his choices for him. But there is something I want people to understand. Addiction was the struggle. My brother was never the enemy. He was still my brother. The same brother I grew up with. The same person my mom and dad loved. The same person whose heart I knew long before addiction became part of our family's story. I didn't always agree with his choices. Sometimes I was angry. Sometimes I was hurt. Sometimes I was so frightened that I didn't know how to put my feelings into words. I hated what addiction was doing to him. I hated what it was taking from his life and the pain it was causing our family. But I never hated him. I never stopped seeing the person behind the addiction. And I never stopped believing that his life had value and purpose. There were moments when I desperately wished my mom and dad were still here. I wanted to pick up the phone and call them. To tell them what was happening. To ask what they thought I should do. To hear their voices and feel that reassurance only a parent can give. But even though they were no longer physically here, I felt them with me. Sometimes I could almost hear what they would have said. Sometimes I felt their spiritual presence so strongly that it seemed as though they were standing beside me. And sometimes I simply felt the responsibility of carrying forward the love they had taught us. I can't fully explain it, but I felt as though I was loving my brother not only with my own heart, but with all the love our family had poured into us over the years. When I prayed for him, I thought about how my mom and dad would have prayed for him. When I tried to help him, I thought about how they would have wanted him to know he was loved. And when I was afraid of losing him, I felt the weight of all the losses our family had already endured. There were times when I felt as though I was fighting to hold on to one of the last pieces of the family life we had all shared. But through it all, I leaned on God. One of my favorite passages in the Bible has always been Ephesians 6, where we are told to put on the whole armor of God. Ephesians 6:13 (KJV) "Wherefore take unto you the whole armour of God, that ye may be able to withstand in the evil day, and having done all, to stand." Those last few words have taken on such a powerful meaning in my life. "And having done all, to stand." Because sometimes standing was all I knew how to do. When I couldn't change my brother's choices, I stood in prayer. When I couldn't understand why things were happening, I stood in faith. When I was afraid of losing him, I held on to hope. And when I felt as though I had nothing left to give, I asked God for the strength to face another day. I won't pretend that I was always strong. I wasn't. There were times I cried. Times I questioned. Times I felt defeated. Times when I wondered how much more my heart could take. But I've learned that faith doesn't mean we never feel afraid or overwhelmed. Sometimes faith means trusting God while we're still frightened, still hurting, and still uncertain about what tomorrow will bring. And I learned something else along the way. Loving someone through addiction doesn't mean you have to approve of their choices, rescue them from every consequence, or sacrifice your own well-being. You can love someone deeply and still establish boundaries. You can be disappointed and still show compassion. You can recognize the damage addiction causes without forgetting the worth of the person struggling. And you can hold on to hope without pretending everything is okay. The Bible tells us in Galatians 6:2 (KJV): "Bear ye one another's burdens, and so fulfil the law of Christ." I believe we are called to help carry one another through life's difficult seasons. Not to take away someone's responsibility for their choices, but to remind them that they are not alone. Through my brother's struggles, I've also seen how quickly society can judge someone battling addiction. People see the mistakes, the relapses, the broken promises, and the consequences. But they don't always see the person underneath. They don't see the family members lying awake at night, praying for another chance. They don't see the fear in a sister's heart when she wonders whether her brother will survive. They don't see the parents, children, spouses, and loved ones who are hurting, too. And they don't always see the potential that still exists within the person struggling. I wish more people understood that addiction does not erase someone's humanity. A person can make terrible decisions and still be worthy of dignity. A person can fall more than once and still have the ability to change. And a person can lose their way without losing their worth. Through everything, I've continued to believe that God has a purpose for my brother's life. And now, as my brother and I work toward establishing No Judgement, a sober living program, I believe something meaningful may grow out of the very experiences that have caused our family so much heartache. We both believe God has placed a calling on his heart to help others who are struggling with addiction. And I believe that with all my heart. I believe his experiences may allow him to reach people who feel misunderstood, ashamed, or hopeless. I believe he may recognize pain in someone else's eyes because he has experienced struggles of his own. I believe that sometimes the person who has walked through the darkest valley can offer understanding and encouragement to someone who is trying to find their way out. And I believe God can use even the most painful chapters of our lives for something good. 2 Corinthians 1:4 (KJV) reminds us that God comforts us in our troubles so that we may comfort others with the comfort we ourselves have received. That Scripture means so much to me. Because perhaps the fear I've experienced can help me understand another sister who is terrified of losing her brother. Perhaps the grief I've carried can help me recognize the pain of another family struggling to hold on. And perhaps the compassion we've learned through our own experiences can become part of the foundation of a place where others find encouragement, stability, accountability, and hope. That is what No Judgement means to me. It isn't about pretending addiction doesn't cause harm. It isn't about excusing destructive behavior or removing accountability. It's about recognizing that people are more than their worst moments. It's about helping people take responsibility for their lives while treating them with compassion and dignity. It's about giving someone who is willing to work toward recovery an opportunity to rebuild. And it's about reminding families that they are not alone in their pain. For me, No Judgement is personal. It's about the brother I almost lost. It's about the parents I wish were still here to walk this journey with us. It's about the family we have always been, the love we were raised with, and the faith that has carried us through more than I ever thought we could endure. It's about the countless families who are praying tonight that someone they love will make it through another day. And it's about believing that even after heartbreak, disappointment, and loss, there can still be purpose. I don't know what tomorrow holds. I don't know every challenge we will face as we work toward making No Judgement a reality. But I know what is in my heart. I want another sister who is afraid of losing her brother to know she isn't alone. I want another mother or father who is praying for their child to know that their pain matters, too. I want families to understand that they can love someone through addiction without losing themselves in the process. And I want people struggling with addiction to know that their lives still matter, that asking for help takes courage, and that their past does not have to define their future. I also want to honor the love my parents gave us. I wish they were here to see what we're trying to accomplish. I wish I could tell them about this vision, hear their thoughts, and share this journey with them. But in my heart, I feel as though a part of them is still walking beside us. And perhaps one of the most beautiful ways we can honor their memory is by extending the same love, compassion, and hope they instilled in us to others who desperately need it. My testimony is not that I saved my brother. I couldn't. My testimony is that through the fear, the tears, the disappointments, and the moments when I thought I might lose him forever, God helped me continue loving him and holding on to hope. My testimony is that even when I couldn't see a way forward, I believed God was not finished writing his story. And my prayer is that the very experiences that nearly took my brother's life may someday become part of what helps someone else reclaim theirs. I have learned that faith doesn't mean we never face storms. Sometimes faith means standing in the middle of the storm, with tears in our eyes, trusting God to give us the strength to make it through. And sometimes, when we have done everything we know how to do, all that remains is to stand. "And having done all, to stand." — Ephesians 6:13 I couldn't fight my brother's battle for him. But I could love him. I could pray for him. I could encourage him toward healing. And I could believe that his life was still worth fighting for. Addiction was the struggle. My brother was never the enemy. He was always the person I loved, the person I prayed for, and the person I desperately wanted to see healed. And today, as we begin this journey with No Judgement, I pray that our family's pain can become someone else's hope. That our experiences can become understanding. That our struggles can become compassion. And that through God's grace, what once brought so much heartbreak may one day help bring healing to others. This is my story. This is my faith. This is my hope. And this is why No Judgement means so much to me. Because every life has worth. Every family deserves compassion. And even in the darkest moments, there is still reason to hope.
